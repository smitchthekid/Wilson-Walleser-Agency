# Strategies for Product Advertising Campaigns

The right advertising strategy depends on two things: what you sell and who else is selling it. A low-margin commodity and a high-margin specialty product need different plans, even if both run on the same platforms. Before you set a budget, you need to understand your margins, your customers' intent, and how crowded the auction is.

## Start with margin, not search volume

It is tempting to read search volume as a measure of opportunity. Volume only tells you how many people typed something. It does not tell you whether they want to buy, or whether you can afford the click.

Start with the numbers inside your business instead:

- **Margin per sale.** What is left after product cost, shipping, and fees?
- **Break-even return.** How much revenue does each ad dollar need to bring back before you lose money?
- **Average order value.** Higher order values can support more expensive clicks.

Those three numbers set the ceiling on what you can bid. A product with thin margins cannot win expensive auctions, no matter how good the ads are.

Intent matters just as much. Someone searching for "how does a standing desk work" is learning. Someone searching for a specific desk model with the word "buy" is shopping. Showing ads to the first group is expensive and rarely pays off.

## Read the competition

You can judge how competitive a market is without guessing. Look at:

- **Cost per click.** Rising costs usually mean more bidders or higher-value customers.
- **Auction insights.** Google Ads shows which competitors appear alongside you and how often.
- **Impression share.** This shows what share of the possible impressions your ads received, which helps you see whether raising a bid or budget could reach more people.
- **The results page itself.** Search a few of your target terms. Notice how much space goes to shopping units, ads, and other features before the first organic result.

## High-competition markets

Competition is steepest where brands are well known, products are in demand, or margins are high. Large margins let competitors bid aggressively, which pushes costs up for everyone.

### Branded search terms

A branded search includes a company name, product line, or trademark. These searches often come from people close to a purchase, so they tend to be valuable.

The line between branded and non-branded is not always clean. Some brand names become the word people use for the whole product category. Treat these terms as a separate segment in your account so you can see what they cost and what they return.

Two rules help:

- **Always protect your own brand terms.** If you do not advertise on your name, a competitor may.
- **Be selective about competitors' brand terms.** Bidding on them can be legal, but platforms limit how you can use another company's trademark in ad copy, and the rules differ by platform. Check each platform's current policy before you try it, and expect the clicks to cost more and convert less than your own brand terms.

### Know when to walk away

Not every click is worth buying. Shoppers who compare prices across ten tabs may never choose you, and your budget pays for clicks, not customers. Put your spend where margin and intent line up, and let competitors overpay for the rest.

## Low-competition markets and new products

When competition is low, the product is usually new or unfamiliar. People do not search for things they do not know exist, so they start with general terms and may find you along the way.

In these markets:

- Start with broad problem-based searches ("how to keep produce fresh longer") and watch which ones lead to sales.
- Keep early budgets modest and learn from the search terms report before you scale.
- Pair ads with a clear product page. A new product needs the page to do the explaining.

Do not assume a quiet market stays quiet. If your product works, others will follow.

## Where small brands win

You will not out-spend a national retailer, and you do not have to. Large advertisers often run on volume and can absorb some poor-fit clicks. Small brands win by being specific.

- **Long-tail searches.** Longer, more specific queries cost less and often show clearer intent.
- **Local and "near me" intent.** If you serve an area, say so in your ads and listings.
- **Alternatives and comparisons.** People searching for alternatives to a product are often ready to switch.
- **Product feed quality.** In Shopping and Performance Max campaigns, your feed does much of the work. Clear titles, accurate product identifiers, good images, and sensible grouping of products affect where and how often your products appear.
- **Helpful content.** A short buyer's guide or comparison page can capture shoppers who are not yet searching by brand.

Competing on price alone is a trap against marketplaces and big retailers. Shoppers also weigh shipping speed, returns, and reviews, so build advantages in those areas before cutting your price.

## Know the limits of each platform

Every ad platform restricts certain products and claims, including alcohol, health claims, financial services, and others. Rules vary by country and change over time. Recreational drugs and many other items cannot be advertised on major platforms, and some categories, such as gambling, need certification before ads can run.

Ads are also sometimes flagged by mistake. If that happens, read the exact policy cited, correct the ad or landing page if there is a real issue, and file an appeal through the platform if there is not. Check the official policy pages for each platform before you plan a campaign around a regulated product.

## Measure what pays

Platform dashboards and your analytics tool often disagree. Each counts and credits conversions differently, and privacy changes and consent rules mean some activity goes unseen.

A few habits keep your reporting honest:

- Track real business outcomes, such as sales and revenue, not just clicks.
- Compare platform-reported results against your actual sales for the same period.
- Set up conversion tracking carefully and respect consent requirements.
- For larger budgets, test whether ads cause sales by pausing them in some regions and comparing results.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best platform for product advertising?

It depends on your product and customer. Search and Shopping ads reach people who are already looking, while social platforms help people discover products they did not know to search for. Our post on the [differences between Google and Facebook advertising](/blog/the-truth-about-google-advertising-differences-between-google-facebook-advertising-strategies) explains how they differ.

### How much should I spend on ads?

Start from your margins and break-even return, not from a round number. The right budget is one you can afford to learn from while the account collects data.

### Can I advertise against my competitors?

Often yes, but platform rules and trademark policies limit how you can do it, and results are mixed. Test carefully and check current policies first.

### Do I need a product feed?

If you sell physical products online, yes. A well-built feed is the foundation of Shopping campaigns.

# Clean energy: Controversial, Contentious, and Disputed

In 2018, legislation coined the "Affordable Clean Energy Act". The data within demonstrates how data is often polarized, skewed and misinterpreted.

## So what is the Affordable Clean Energy (ACE) Act?

"The EPA’s new plan would direct states to set modest limits on emissions that can be achieved with relatively minor upgrades to existing coal power plants. To facilitate those changes, the EPA proposes to rewrite its [New Source Review (NSR)](https://www.epa.gov/nsr) rule, which regulates pollution from new facilities or modified sources."

More specifically, the NSR protects air quality when factories, industrial boilers, and power plants are newly built or modified. It ensures new and modified industrial processes are "[as clean as possible](https://www.epa.gov/nsr)" and maintain the balance of industrial expansion with new advances in pollution control.

Critics suggest the EPA’s new ACE rule is a coal lifeline ‘disguised as a greenhouse gas regulation.' A recent New York Times article declared the bill as a [plot to de-emphasize premature deaths caused by coal-based pollution](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/05/20/climate/epa-air-pollution-deaths.html).

Proponents suggest that if these regulations would allow coal plants to continue operations for a measure of 10 years, as opposed to only 5 under current regulation, the responses of naysayers become moot.

The EPA contends that its new suggestions address market failures which unnecessarily raise energy costs and conflict with energy trends that are already reducing C02 emissions. The ACE plan is an effort to harmonize regulations by 'making it clear that states have broad discretion in establishing and applying emissions standards consistent with the BSER "best system of emission reduction".

Generally, these regulatory changes are aimed at reducing the cost burden of those adversely impacted by the Clean Energy Act of 2009. In large, the CEA bill impacted traditional energy industries and prioritized new energy markets focused on efficiency and renewed resources.

## Reading Between the Lines

The EPA's latest revision in targets for C02 and SOx emissions from coal-powered plants also includes self-titled "omissions".

EPA documents state:

"There are important impacts that EPA could not monetize. Due to current data and modeling limitations, our estimates of the benefit impacts from reducing CO2 emissions **do not include** important impacts like ocean acidification or potential tipping points in natural or managed ecosystems. Ancillary benefits from changing direct exposure to SO2, NOX, as well as ecosystem changes and visibility impairment, from changes in these pollutants are also omitted."

## Conclusions

By large, the language of the ACE analysis underpins a fiscal proposal, not a clean energy plan.

As written, ACE is not a bill characterized by reductions in fossil fuel pollution; its an extension of short-term profitability for coal plants that otherwise would need to reduce emissions or otherwise cease operation. Upgrading already existing coal-fired plants is expensive, which certainly impacts the working class and vaporize coal jobs.

This bill has a good intention: to extend the working years of existing coal plants and slow economic impact.

Mathematically, it's all straight forward. Studies conducted by the Health Effects Institute, an EPA funded organization, propose that the ACE policy revisions will not reduce overall C02 or SOx levels in the coming decades.

They conclude,

"Although the ACE reduces the emissions intensity of coal plants, it is expected to increase the number of operating coal plants and amount of coal-fired electricity generation, with 28% of model plants showing higher CO2 emissions in 2030 compared to no policy.

As a result, the ACE only modestly reduces national power sector CO2 emissions and increases CO2 emissions by up to 8.7% in 18 states plus the District of Columbia in 2030 compared to no [revision of] policy. We also find that the ACE increases SO2 and NOX emissions in 19 states and 20 states plus DC, respectively, in 2030 compared to no policy, with implications for air quality and public health…"

"Of the 333 model coal plants that would be in operation in 2030 under no policy, 93 of those (or 28%) are projected to have higher total CO2 emissions under the ACE."
